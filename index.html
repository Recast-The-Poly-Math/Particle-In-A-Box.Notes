Quantum Mechanics • First Canonical Problem

Particle-In-A-Box.Notes

Explore how the Schrödinger equation, boundary conditions, normalization, and energy quantization fit together in one of the simplest quantum systems.

1. Physical setup

A particle of mass m is trapped between two perfectly rigid walls at x = 0 and x = L.

V(x) = 0 for 0 < x < L,    and    V(x) = ∞ outside the box.

Because the particle cannot exist outside the walls, its wavefunction must vanish at the boundaries:

ψ(0) = 0,    ψ(L) = 0

2. Solve the Schrödinger equation

Inside the box, V = 0:
−(ℏ²/2m) ψ″(x) = E ψ(x)
Rearrange:
ψ″(x) + k² ψ(x) = 0,    k² = 2mE/ℏ²
General solution:
ψ(x) = A sin(kx) + B cos(kx)
Apply ψ(0) = 0, which forces B = 0:
ψ(x) = A sin(kx)
Apply ψ(L) = 0:
sin(kL) = 0 ⇒ kL = nπ
where n = 1, 2, 3, ...

3. Quantized energies

Since kₙ = nπ/L, substitute into E = ℏ²k²/(2m):

Eₙ = n²π²ℏ² / (2mL²)

Only discrete energies are allowed. The particle cannot have an arbitrary energy while remaining in a stationary state.

4. Where does √(2/L) come from?

The differential equation leaves the amplitude A undetermined. Quantum mechanics fixes it using probability normalization:

∫₀ᴸ |ψ(x)|² dx = 1
Substitute ψ(x) = A sin(nπx/L):
A² ∫₀ᴸ sin²(nπx/L) dx = 1
The integral evaluates to:
∫₀ᴸ sin²(nπx/L) dx = L/2
Therefore:
A²(L/2) = 1 ⇒ A² = 2/L
Choose the usual positive real normalization:
A = √(2/L)

Normalized state: ψₙ(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L)

5. Exponential form

Sines and cosines are equivalent to complex exponentials through Euler's formula.

e^(ikx) = cos(kx) + i sin(kx)
sin(kx) = [e^(ikx) − e^(−ikx)] / (2i)

Thus the standing-wave solution can also be viewed as a superposition of two complex traveling waves moving in opposite directions.

Main takeaway

The differential equation gives the family of possible wave shapes. The boundary conditions select the allowed wavelengths. Those wavelengths quantize the energy. Finally, normalization fixes the amplitude.
